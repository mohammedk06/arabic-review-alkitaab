I Live in New York City
◆ WHAT THIS LESSON LOCKS IN
The building blocks of the noun phrase: grammatical gender (المذكّر والمؤنث), the nisba adjective (النسبة) that turns a place into “belonging to that place,” the interrogative particles for asking questions, and the definite article الـ. Everything about agreement later hangs off gender.

📎 Notes on usage (from the lesson)
1. “My father / my mother” = the word + the suffix ـي. Add the possessive ـي to make it “my” (formally taught in Lesson 3):
Base word+ ـي (my)Meaning
والِدوالِديmy father
والِدةوالِدتيmy mother
Note the ة of والِدة becomes ت when the suffix attaches → والِدتي.
2. Gender lives on the noun — there is no neutral “it”. You must choose هو or هي by the noun’s gender even for objects:

2 · Grammar القواعد
2a · Gender — المذكّر والمؤنث
Every Arabic noun and adjective carries gender: مُذَكَّر (masculine) or مُؤَنَّث (feminine). Two categories behave differently:
CategoryRule
Human nouns (مها، أستاذة، صاحب)gender follows the actual person
Nonhuman nouns (كِتاب، مَدينة)each has a fixed gender you memorize
The single best gender clue is the تاء مربوطة (ة) — it almost always marks feminine (مَدينة، جِنسِيّة). Adjectives must agree with the noun’s gender: الأدَب الإنجليزيّ (both m), والِدتي فِلَسطينِيّة (both f).
⚠ GENDER EXCEPTIONS TO MEMORIZE
• A few masculine words end in ة anyway — e.g. خَليفة (caliph).
• A few feminine words have no ة — e.g. شَمس (sun), أُمّ (mother), أرض (earth).
2b · The nisba adjective — النِّسبة
A nisba turns a noun (usually a place) into an adjective of origin/affiliation by adding a suffix:
GenderSuffixExample
مذكّر‑ِيّمصر → مِصريّ
مؤنّث‑ِيّةمصر → مِصريّة
How to build one — strip, then add:
Place nounStep 1: remove ة / الـ / final ا or يStep 2: add ‑ِيّ / ‑ِيّة
أمريكاأمريك‑أمريكيّ / أمريكيّة
مَكتَبةمَكتَب‑مَكتَبيّ / مَكتَبيّة
الأُردُنّأُردُن‑أُردُنيّ / أُردُنيّة
لُبنانلُبنان‑لُبنانيّ / لُبنانيّة
Many family surnames are frozen nisbas naming a hometown: الفاسيّ (from Fez), البغداديّ (from Baghdad), المصريّ (from Cairo). See General Review › Nisba adjectives.
2c · Interrogatives — السؤال
The question particle comes at the front of the sentence, just like English. The core فصحى set:
مَن؟
who?
مَن هذا؟
ما؟ / ماذا؟
what?
ما هذا؟
أين؟
where?
أين تَسكُن؟
كيف؟
how?
كيف الأخبار؟
أيّ؟
which?
أيّ لُغة؟
كَم؟
how many?
كم سَنة؟
Spoken dialects swap some of these (Levantine وين = أين, شو = ما; Egyptian إيه = ما, فين = أين) — but the structure (particle first) is identical.
2d · The definite article — الـ
Prefix الـ makes a noun definite: طالِب (“a student”) → الطالِب (“the student”). Two things to remember:
PointExample
Arabic uses الـ more than English — abstract/generic nouns are definiteأدرُس الأدَب = “I study literature” (no “the” in English)
Sun letters assimilate the ل in pronunciation (spelling unchanged)الشَّمس → ash-shams

3 · Culture & readings context, light review
Story: مها (Maha) introduces herself — a young woman living in New York City; the lesson threads identity, nationality, and family.

Culture — Arabic names: traditional full names chain ancestors with ابن (“son of,” written بن between two names), e.g. عَليّ بن العَبّاس بن جُرَيج (ابن الرومي). People are also tagged by a nisba (hometown). A name like “Abdul” is only half — عَبد (“servant of”) must be followed by an attribute of God: عبد الله، عبد القادر، عبد الحكيم.

Titles: Arabs address each other by first name plus a title (دكتور محمد) unless close friends.

Note: most Arab women keep their father’s family name for life rather than taking a husband’s.
4 · Common pitfalls watch these in the drill
1Reaching for a neutral “it” — there isn’t one; pick هو/هي by the noun’s gender.
2Assuming ة = always feminine — mostly true, but خَليفة is masculine and شَمس/أُمّ are feminine without ة.
3Building a nisba without stripping first — remember to drop ة / الـ / final ا before adding ‑ِيّ (مكتبة → مكتبيّ, not مكتبةيّ).
4Forgetting الـ on abstract nouns — أدرُس الأدَب, not أدرُس أدَب.
5Question particle placement — it goes first, never buried mid-sentence.
